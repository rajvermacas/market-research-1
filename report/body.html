The Dip Buy Audit

A widely-shared RSI(2) dip-buying strategy claims an 83% win rate and $335K since 2009 on one ES and one NQ contract. Every headline number checks out. The equity curve behind them still tells you something that isn't true — and the effect largely fails to survive the trip to India.

Replicates

The numbers are honest

Overstated

RULES

What was tested

Reconstructed verbatim from the post, with nothing added

  1. RSI(2) on daily closes
  2. Buy the close when RSI(2) < 10 and price > 200 day SMA
  3. Sell the close when RSI(2) > 70, or after 10 sessions, whichever comes first
  4. Long only. ES and NQ, 1 contract each. No stop.

Originally from Larry Connors & Cesar Alvarez, Short Term Trading Strategies That Work.

Wilder's RSI. $4 round-turn commission plus one tick of slippage per side, as the post specifies. ES at $50/point, NQ at $20/point. Yahoo daily futures, back-adjusted for quarterly roll gaps — see finding 5.

01 / 06

The claims reproduce

Independently, from raw data, without seeing the author's code

This is the part that usually fails. It doesn't here. Rebuilding the strategy from the four rules and running it on daily ES and NQ history lands within a few percent of every published figure — and lands on the green-year count exactly.

The edge is also statistically real, not a run of luck. Mean return per trade is +0.435% of notional across 298 trades, t = 5.00, p < 10−6. Replacing the RSI signal with random entry days — same 200-SMA filter, same number of trades, same holding periods, 2,000 simulations — the actual result lands at the 99.9th percentile on ES and the 98.3rd on NQ. The timing is doing work that being long alone does not explain.

And it holds out of sample. The published curve starts in 2009; run the same rules across 2001–2008, all the futures history available, and it returns 91 trades at a 75.8% win rate and a 2.23 profit factor through the dot-com bust and the financial crisis.

The hockey stick is the index, not the edge

The single most important thing the chart gets wrong

The published equity curve bends sharply upward after 2021 — the visual signature of a strategy hitting its stride. It is nothing of the kind. One NQ contract is a $590,000 position today and was a $130,000 position in 2012. Holding size fixed at one contract while the index quadruples converts a flat edge into an exponential chart.

03 / 06

It captures 38% of doing nothing

In the same dollars-per-contract unit the post uses

If dollars per contract is the yardstick, it has to be applied consistently. Buying one ES and one NQ in January 2009 and never touching them again returns $818,063 by August 2026. The strategy returns $314,790.

The fair defence is exposure: the strategy is in the market on only 15% of sessions and holds an average of 4.5 days, so per unit of time at risk it is far more efficient, and the 200-SMA filter kept it almost entirely out of 2022 — four trades all year. That is a genuine strength. It is also not what a $335K headline over a rising equity curve communicates.

04 / 06

An 83% win rate that loses more than it wins

The payoff shape a win rate is designed to hide

Win rate is the headline because it is the most flattering number this strategy produces. The average winner is $2,352. The average loser is $3,588 — 1.53 times larger. The strategy is profitable because it wins four times out of five, not because its wins are big.

That asymmetry is structural, not bad luck. Exits split two ways: 284 trades closed on the RSI target for a combined +$438,428; 14 trades ran out the 10-session clock while the dip kept dipping, for −$123,638. Those fourteen trades are the entire risk profile. With no stop, "no stop" is the risk model.

05 / 06

The drawdown, and what it costs to survive

Marked to market daily, not on closed trades

A closed-trade equity curve books a loss only on the day you exit, which flatters any strategy that holds losers. Marked to market every session, the deepest hole is −$47,531, dug between February and May 2025 and not filled until October — 251 days underwater, in the most recent third of the record.

That sets the capital. Initial margin on one ES and one NQ at current levels runs about $58,600; funding the drawdown on top means an account near $106,000. $314,790 over 17.7 years on that base is roughly 16.8% a year. A real return, and a very different sentence from "$335K".

06 / 06

Two things the data itself adds

Neither is the author's doing; both change the total

Roll gaps are worth 8% of the profit. Yahoo's ES=F and NQ=F stitch each quarterly contract to the next at expiry without back-adjusting, so every roll injects a price jump of roughly +0.5% to +1.5% in contango that no position ever earned. A long-only backtest collects them as free money. Removing them with a Panama adjustment takes $342,275 down to $314,790 — $27,485 that was never there. Anyone reproducing this on Yahoo data without adjusting will land near the higher number.

"Buy the close" is not quite executable. The signal is RSI(2) computed on the very close you are filled at — you cannot know it until the print. Taking the next open instead, the honest bound:

INDIA

Does it work on the Nifty?

The hardest out-of-sample test available: a different market, same rules

Everything above concerns two US contracts. A mean-reversion effect that is real should show up wherever the same forces do — so the identical rules were run on the two most-traded NSE index contracts, Nifty 50 and Nifty Bank, over 2008–Aug 2026.

It does not travel. The effect is present in the raw prices and too weak to trade after what it costs to trade it in India.

Why it breaks: the cost stack

The Indian edge starts smaller and is then taxed much harder. Securities transaction tax alone is 0.02% of the sell side, and — the piece most backtests miss — a long futures position gives up the basis as it converges. At a ~6.5% repo rate against a ~1.3% dividend yield, holding for the strategy's average 4.8 sessions costs 0.10% of notional before anything else.

The random-entry test is more damning than the p-values. In the US, the RSI(2) signal beats randomly chosen entry days above the 200 SMA at the 99.9th percentile — the timing is unambiguously doing work. In India the same test puts Nifty at the 80th percentile and Nifty Bank at the 60th. At the 60th percentile you are not timing anything; you are just long an index that rose.

Verdict for Indian traders: the published edge is a US phenomenon at these parameters. On the Nifty it survives as a marginal gross effect that the STT-and-carry stack consumes almost half of; on the Nifty Bank it does not survive at all. Anyone porting these rules to NSE futures should expect a profit factor near 1.2 and a drawdown roughly the size of their lifetime profit — not 83% winners.

NSE EQUITIES

And on single stocks?

340 liquid Nifty 500 names, 27,892 trades, 2008 – Aug 2026

Index futures are one thing; NSE cash equities are where most Indian retail traders would actually run this. So the same four rules were applied to every liquid Nifty 500 constituent — 340 stocks clearing ₹5 crore of median daily turnover — at a fixed ₹1 lakh per position.

The headline looks emphatic. 27,892 trades, 67.2% winners, +0.545% per trade before costs, t = 18.0. A t-statistic of 18 is not a fluke. But it is measuring the wrong thing, and two numbers dismantle it.

The whole finding in one line. The real timing edge is 0.075% a trade. Delivery-equity round-trip friction is 0.32% — 4.3× the edge it is trying to capture. Even as stock futures, at 0.126%, costs are still 1.7× the edge.

The strategy still finishes positive after costs, at +0.222% a trade — but so would randomly buying uptrending stocks, at +0.147%. You are paying 0.32% in tax and spread to harvest 0.075% of signal on top of a drift anyone can have for free.

The random-entry benchmark also settles the survivorship question. Today's Nifty 500 is a list of companies that made it, so a long-only backtest on it is flattered — but the benchmark trades that same survivor list, so the bias inflates both numbers equally. Only the 0.075% gap is survivorship-neutral.

You cannot take the trades anyway

The 27,892 figure assumes unlimited capital. RSI(2) < 10 is a market-wide condition: when India sells off, hundreds of stocks trip it at once. The uncapped book holds a median of 24 positions, 71 at the 90th percentile, and peaked at 219 open positions on 26 October 2023 — ₹2.19 crore deployed at ₹1 lakh each.

Cap it at something a person can actually run, and the result collapses:

And single stocks bring a tail that indices do not. The average loss is −4.61% against an average win of +2.88%. 11.3% of trades went more than 10% underwater and 1.6% went past 20%, with no stop to intervene. The worst was ADANIENT in January 2023 — entered on the dip, −70.6% at its low, closed by the 10-session clock at −54.5%. The 3,267 time-stop exits, 11.7% of trades, lost ₹2.78 crore between them while the 24,625 target exits made ₹3.40 crore.

Verdict for NSE equities: the effect is statistically real and economically negligible. It is 0.075% a trade of genuine signal sitting under 0.32% of cost, in a strategy that wants 24 simultaneous positions and hands you a 50% drawdown for a 3.8% annual return when you size it like a human. A single-stock dip buy with no stop also carries −70% excursions that no index version ever will.

STRESS

Does it survive being poked?

Mostly yes — with one sensitivity worth knowing

A strategy that only works at its published parameters is a curve fit. This one isn't: every perturbation below stays profitable, and most stay near the original profit factor. That is a real point in its favour.

The exception is which RSI you use. Wilder's smoothing — Connors' definition, and the one used throughout this audit — gives a 2.35 profit factor. The simple-average variant that several charting packages default to gives 1.62 on two and a half times the trades. Same rules, different indicator library, materially different strategy.

VERDICT

What it actually is

A well-behaved short-term mean-reversion effect in index futures, worth about 0.44% of notional per trade, roughly 17 trades a year, in the market 15% of the time. It is statistically significant, it holds out of sample back to 2001, it is robust to its parameters, and the person who posted it reported its numbers accurately.

What the presentation oversells is not the arithmetic. It is the unit. Dollars per contract on an index that has quadrupled is the most flattering measure available, and it turns eighteen years of a constant edge into a chart that looks like a discovery compounding. Priced properly — against the capital it needs, against the drawdown it puts you through, against simply holding the same two contracts — it is a decent overlay returning something like 17% a year, with a rare but real left tail that no stop protects.

The one thing to check before trading it: which RSI your platform computes. Wilder's gives you the strategy above. The simple-average variant gives you a different, meaningfully worse one under the same name.

Method & caveats

Daily OHLC for ES=F, NQ=F, ^GSPC and ^NDX from the Yahoo Finance chart API, through 28 August 2026. Futures series back-adjusted for quarterly roll gaps, sized as the portion of each roll-day move the matching cash index does not explain. Wilder's RSI(2); 200-session simple moving average; $4 round-turn commission plus one tick per side unless noted. Entry and exit both at the daily close, as the rules specify. Position size is fixed at one contract throughout, also as specified. Significance tested by one-sample t-test on per-trade notional returns and by 2,000-path random-entry resampling matched on trade count, holding period and regime filter.

India: no usable continuous NSE futures series exists, so the Indian runs use the Nifty 50, Nifty Bank, Nifty IT and Sensex cash indices as futures proxies and charge the long-futures cost of carry explicitly at 5.2% a year (a ~6.5% repo rate less a ~1.3% dividend yield) over each holding period. Transaction costs model the full statutory stack — STT at 0.02% of the sell side, exchange and SEBI charges, stamp duty and GST, about 0.0257% of notional per round turn — plus ₹47 of brokerage and one index point of slippage per side (three on Nifty Bank). Rupee figures use current NSE lot sizes throughout, 75 for Nifty and 30 for Nifty Bank; percentage figures are lot-independent and are the primary unit for any cross-market comparison.

NSE equities: all Nifty 500 constituents whose Yahoo history is available and whose median daily turnover since 2008 clears ₹5 crore — 340 of 485 downloaded names. Prices are split and bonus adjusted. Positions are a fixed ₹1 lakh, so no result depends on share price level. Delivery friction is modelled at 0.323% per round trip: STT 0.1% on each side, exchange charge 0.00297% a side, stamp duty 0.015% on the buy, SEBI and GST, plus 0.05% of slippage per side. The stock-futures column swaps STT for the 0.02% sell-side rate. The universe is today's index membership, so absolute returns carry survivorship bias; the random-entry benchmark trades the identical list, which is what makes the 0.075% timing gap survivorship-neutral. Capped-portfolio results break ties on a signal date by lowest entry RSI, which is known at the signal and introduces no look-ahead.

Yahoo daily futures data is not a tick-accurate settlement series; treat P&L as accurate to a few percent. Margin figures are approximate CME initial requirements at current index levels. Full code, trade-by-trade output and the raw result files are in the repository under src/ and output/.
